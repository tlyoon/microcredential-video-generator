## V01 — What Does a Measurement Really Mean?

### V01S01 — A number alone is not a measurement

**On-screen**

- “12.4” is not yet physics.
- Meaning needs a quantity, unit and context.
- The laboratory result must be interpretable.

**Narration script**

Start with a simple number: twelve point four. By itself, it tells us almost nothing. Is it twelve point four millimetres, seconds, volts, or grams? A measurement becomes scientific only when the number is attached to a physical quantity, a unit, and enough context for someone else to understand how reliable it is. This is the foundation of everything else in the laboratory.

### V01S02 — The minimum complete result

**On-screen**

- Measured value.
- Correct unit.
- Uncertainty or resolution context.
- Short method statement when needed.

**Narration script**

A useful laboratory result has a few essential parts. First, the measured value. Second, the unit, because the unit defines the physical scale. Third, a statement of uncertainty, or at least the resolution context when uncertainty has not yet been fully evaluated. Sometimes the method also matters, because two methods can measure the same quantity with different limitations.

### V01S03 — Resolution is not the whole uncertainty

**On-screen**

- Resolution: smallest readable step.
- Uncertainty: credible doubt in the result.
- Human timing, alignment and calibration also matter.

**Narration script**

Students often treat the smallest scale division as the uncertainty. That is only a starting point. Resolution tells us the smallest step that the instrument displays or allows us to read. Uncertainty is broader: it is the credible range of doubt in the final result. Reaction time, alignment, calibration, zero error and the stability of the object being measured can all contribute.

### V01S04 — How to report a measured value

**On-screen**

- Use: symbol equals value plus or minus uncertainty, unit.
- Keep value and uncertainty in compatible decimal places.
- Never omit the unit.

**Narration script**

A standard reporting form is: symbol equals best value plus or minus uncertainty, followed by the unit. The unit belongs to both the value and the uncertainty. The value and uncertainty should be rounded consistently, so that the last quoted digit of the value is meaningful relative to the uncertainty. This form makes the measurement usable by another person.

### V01S05 — Check: what is missing?

**On-screen**

- A student writes: L equals 24.6.
- Can this be accepted as a lab result?
- What information must be added?

**Narration script**

Pause here and decide what is missing from the statement L equals twenty four point six. The number may look precise, but without a unit and uncertainty context it is not a complete laboratory result. A better answer must identify the unit and provide a justified uncertainty or measurement limitation.

### V01S06 — Three habits from the start

**On-screen**

- Always attach units.
- Always think about uncertainty.
- Report results so another person can interpret them.

**Narration script**

The habit to build from the first lab is simple. Never write a naked number. Attach the correct unit, think about the uncertainty, and report the result in a way that another person can interpret without guessing. Good experimental physics begins with that discipline.

## V02 — Can You Trust Your Measurement?

### V02S01 — Close readings can still be wrong

**On-screen**

- Repeated readings may agree.
- Agreement is precision, not necessarily accuracy.
- Bias can move the whole set.

**Narration script**

Imagine repeated measurements that agree beautifully with one another. That feels reassuring, but it does not prove the result is correct. Close agreement describes precision. Accuracy asks whether the result is close to the accepted or true value. A systematic bias can shift every reading in the same direction while still leaving the readings very consistent.

### V02S02 — Accuracy versus precision

**On-screen**

- Accuracy: closeness to accepted value.
- Precision: closeness among repeated values.
- A result can be precise but inaccurate.

**Narration script**

Accuracy and precision answer different questions. Accuracy compares the result with an accepted value or physical truth. Precision compares the spread of repeated measurements. A precise result has small scatter, but it may still be inaccurate if the instrument has a zero offset or the method contains a bias.

### V02S03 — Random effects create scatter

**On-screen**

- Reaction time.
- Reading variation.
- Environmental fluctuation.
- Reduced by repetition and averaging.

**Narration script**

Random effects are changes that push individual readings up and down unpredictably. Reaction time, small reading differences and environmental fluctuations are common examples. Repetition helps because random deviations partly cancel when we average many readings. The scatter of the readings then tells us how repeatable the measurement is.

### V02S04 — Systematic effects shift the result

**On-screen**

- Zero error.
- Calibration error.
- Parallax or alignment bias.
- Not removed just by repetition.

**Narration script**

Systematic effects are more dangerous because they shift the result in a consistent way. A balance with a zero error, a ruler viewed from the wrong angle, or a poorly calibrated sensor can bias all readings. Taking more repeated readings may make the average more stable, but it does not automatically remove the bias.

### V02S05 — What to do when trust is uncertain

**On-screen**

- Repeat readings to estimate scatter.
- Check zero and calibration.
- Compare with reference or expected trend.
- Record suspected limitations.

**Narration script**

A good experimental response uses both repetition and checking. Repeat readings help quantify random scatter. Zero checks, calibration checks and alignment checks look for systematic effects. If a reference value or expected trend exists, compare against it. Most importantly, record the limitation specifically instead of hiding it under vague phrases like human error.

### V02S06 — Trust requires two questions

**On-screen**

- Are repeated readings close together?
- Are they close to the correct value?
- What could bias all readings?

**Narration script**

To judge trustworthiness, ask two questions. Are the repeated readings close together? That is precision. Are they close to the correct or accepted value? That is accuracy. Then ask the third practical question: what could have shifted all readings together? That is where systematic effects enter.

## V03 — From Raw Data to a Reliable Result

### V03S01 — Raw data are evidence

**On-screen**

- Do not rewrite observations to look neat.
- Keep units, instruments and conditions.
- Corrections must be traceable.

**Narration script**

In the laboratory, raw data are evidence. They are not decorations to be cleaned after the experiment. If a reading looks inconvenient, the first response is not to erase it. Keep the observation, unit, instrument and condition. Any correction or exclusion must be traceable and scientifically justified.

### V03S02 — The mean estimates the central value

**On-screen**

- Repeated readings give a set.
- Mean gives the best central estimate.
- Scatter shows repeatability.

**Narration script**

When we repeat a measurement under similar conditions, the mean gives our best estimate of the central value. But the mean alone is not enough. The spread of individual readings around the mean tells us how repeatable the measurement was. A result with a mean but no scatter information hides the quality of the data.

### V03S03 — Two different uncertainty ideas

**On-screen**

- Sample standard deviation: spread of readings.
- Standard uncertainty of the mean: uncertainty in the mean.
- More readings reduce uncertainty of the mean.

**Narration script**

There are two related but different quantities. The sample standard deviation describes the scatter of individual readings. The standard uncertainty of the mean describes how well the mean itself is known. Because averaging improves the estimate of the mean, the uncertainty of the mean decreases with the square root of the number of repeated readings.

### V03S04 — Outliers need evidence, not dislike

**On-screen**

- Do not remove a value because it is inconvenient.
- Look for a documented cause.
- Report the rule or reason.

**Narration script**

An unusual value is not automatically wrong. It might reveal a real problem with the experiment, or it might be part of the natural scatter. Removing it is defensible only when there is evidence, such as a documented slip, wrong range, instrument disturbance, or a clear procedural mistake. The reason must be stated.

### V03S05 — A responsible data-treatment path

**On-screen**

1. Keep all raw readings.
2. Identify possible causes of anomalies.
3. Decide using a stated rule.
4. Report mean and uncertainty.

**Narration script**

A responsible path is sequential. First, keep every raw reading. Second, inspect whether any unusual value has a physical or procedural explanation. Third, decide according to a stated rule rather than personal preference. Finally, report the mean together with a justified uncertainty. This protects both the data and the conclusion.

### V03S06 — Data treatment must be auditable

**On-screen**

- Raw observations remain visible.
- Calculations are reproducible.
- Exclusions require reasons.

**Narration script**

The central message is that data treatment must be auditable. Another person should be able to follow your raw observations, reproduce your calculations, and understand any decision to exclude or correct data. Reliability comes not only from the final number, but from the traceable path to that number.

## V04 — Understanding Uncertainty

### V04S01 — Uncertainty is not a confession of failure

**On-screen**

- Every measured result has doubt.
- Uncertainty quantifies that doubt.
- It makes comparison possible.

**Narration script**

Uncertainty is not a confession that the experiment failed. It is the honest numerical expression of doubt in a measured result. Without uncertainty, we cannot judge whether two results agree, whether a discrepancy matters, or whether an improvement in the experiment is meaningful.

### V04S02 — Absolute uncertainty keeps the unit

**On-screen**

- Same unit as the measured value.
- Shows the size of the doubt directly.
- Example form: 2.46 plus or minus 0.03 metres.

**Narration script**

Absolute uncertainty has the same unit as the measured value. If a length is reported in metres, its absolute uncertainty is also in metres. This form is useful because it shows the direct size of the doubt in the same physical scale as the measurement.

### V04S03 — Relative and percentage uncertainty compare scale

**On-screen**

- Relative uncertainty: uncertainty divided by value.
- Percentage uncertainty: relative uncertainty times 100 percent.
- Useful for comparing different quantities.

**Narration script**

Relative uncertainty compares the uncertainty with the size of the measured value. Percentage uncertainty expresses the same idea as a percentage. This is especially useful when comparing quantities of different sizes or units. A one millimetre uncertainty is large for a small object but negligible for a building.

### V04S04 — Error is not the same as uncertainty

**On-screen**

- Error: difference from true or reference value.
- Uncertainty: estimated doubt before knowing truth.
- Percentage difference is not percentage uncertainty.

**Narration script**

A common mistake is to use error and uncertainty as if they mean the same thing. Error refers to a difference from a true or reference value. Uncertainty is the estimated doubt in the measurement. A percentage difference from a reference value is therefore not the same quantity as percentage uncertainty.

### V04S05 — Rounding should match meaning

**On-screen**

- Do not quote unjustified digits.
- Round uncertainty first.
- Round value to the same decimal place as uncertainty.

**Narration script**

Rounding is part of scientific meaning. Too many digits imply a level of confidence the experiment does not support. A useful habit is to round the uncertainty first, then round the measured value to the same decimal place. This keeps the reported result honest and readable.

### V04S06 — Use the right uncertainty language

**On-screen**

- Absolute: size of doubt with unit.
- Relative or percentage: size of doubt compared with value.
- Error: comparison with truth or reference.

**Narration script**

The key is to use the correct language. Absolute uncertainty tells the size of the doubt with a unit. Relative or percentage uncertainty tells how large that doubt is compared with the value. Error describes comparison with a reference or true value. Mixing these ideas leads to weak conclusions.

## V05 — Propagation of Uncertainty

### V05S01 — Calculated results inherit uncertainty

**On-screen**

- Input measurements are uncertain.
- Calculated quantities cannot be exact.
- Uncertainty must travel through the formula.

**Narration script**

Most laboratory results are not measured directly. We measure several quantities and calculate the final result from them. If the inputs are uncertain, the output must also be uncertain. Propagation of uncertainty is the method for carrying that uncertainty through the calculation instead of pretending the final number is exact.

### V05S02 — Addition and subtraction: absolute uncertainties matter

**On-screen**

- Use absolute uncertainty contributions.
- Independent contributions combine in quadrature.
- Same unit as the result.

**Narration script**

For addition and subtraction, the natural uncertainty scale is absolute. If two independent measured quantities are added or subtracted, their absolute uncertainty contributions are combined in quadrature. The result is an absolute uncertainty with the same unit as the calculated quantity.

### V05S03 — Multiplication and division: relative uncertainties matter

**On-screen**

- Use fractional or percentage contributions.
- Independent relative contributions combine in quadrature.
- Convert back to absolute uncertainty at the end.

**Narration script**

For multiplication and division, the natural scale is relative uncertainty. Each input contributes through its fractional uncertainty. After combining the relative contributions, multiply by the final value to recover the absolute uncertainty. This is why percentage uncertainty is so useful in product and quotient calculations.

### V05S04 — Powers multiply relative uncertainty

**On-screen**

- If y depends on x squared, the relative contribution doubles.
- If y depends on x cubed, it triples.
- Powers can make one measurement dominate.

**Narration script**

Powers deserve special attention. If a result depends on a measurement squared, that measurement’s relative uncertainty contribution is doubled. If it is cubed, the contribution is tripled. This is why measuring a diameter poorly can strongly affect calculations involving area or volume.

### V05S05 — Look for the dominant contribution

**On-screen**

- Calculate each contribution.
- Compare magnitudes before blaming everything.
- Improve the measurement that matters most.

**Narration script**

Propagation is not just arithmetic. It also tells us what limits the experiment. After computing each contribution, compare their sizes. If one contribution dominates, improving a tiny contribution will not noticeably improve the final result. The best improvement targets the measurement that controls the uncertainty budget.

### V05S06 — Check: squared quantity

**On-screen**

- A result depends on d squared.
- The relative uncertainty in d is 2 percent.
- What is d’s relative contribution to the result?

**Narration script**

Here is a quick check. If a calculated result depends on d squared, and d has a relative uncertainty of two percent, d contributes four percent to the relative uncertainty of that result. The exponent doubles the contribution.

### V05S07 — Propagation connects formula to reliability

**On-screen**

- Sums and differences: absolute form.
- Products, quotients and powers: relative form.
- The largest contribution guides improvement.

**Narration script**

The practical rule is this. For sums and differences, think in absolute uncertainty. For products, quotients and powers, think in relative uncertainty. Then use the contribution sizes to decide what actually limits the experiment. Propagation turns a formula into an uncertainty-aware result.

## V06 — Finding What Limits Your Experiment

### V06S01 — Not every improvement improves the result

**On-screen**

- Better equipment costs time.
- Some measurements barely affect the final uncertainty.
- Find the limiting contribution first.

**Narration script**

Students often assume that any more precise instrument will improve the experiment. That is not always true. If a contribution is already tiny, improving it further changes almost nothing. An uncertainty budget helps identify the bottleneck: the measurement that actually limits the final result.

### V06S02 — An uncertainty budget separates contributions

**On-screen**

- List each measured input.
- Compute its contribution to the result.
- Compare contributions on the same scale.

**Narration script**

An uncertainty budget separates the final uncertainty into contributions from the measured inputs. Each contribution must be put onto a comparable scale. Once they are comparable, you can see whether the mass, length, diameter, time, voltage or another measurement is controlling the reliability of the result.

### V06S03 — Density shows why formula structure matters

**On-screen**

- Density depends on mass and volume.
- Cylinder volume depends strongly on diameter.
- A small diameter uncertainty can dominate.

**Narration script**

A density experiment is a good example. Density is mass divided by volume. For a cylinder, the volume depends on the diameter squared, so the diameter uncertainty receives extra weight. Even when the diameter looks like an ordinary length measurement, the formula can make it the dominant source of uncertainty.

### V06S04 — Interpret the largest term physically

**On-screen**

- Do not stop at the final uncertainty.
- Ask which measurement produced it.
- Connect the answer to apparatus and method.

**Narration script**

The final uncertainty is not the end of the analysis. The next question is physical: which measurement produced most of it? If the diameter term dominates, the conclusion is not simply that the uncertainty is large. The conclusion is that the diameter measurement method, instrument or alignment needs the most attention.

### V06S05 — Check: what should be improved?

**On-screen**

- Mass contribution: small.
- Diameter contribution: large.
- Which measurement should be improved first?

**Narration script**

If the mass contribution is small but the diameter contribution is large, improving the balance is the wrong priority. The first improvement should target the diameter measurement. That might mean a better instrument, better alignment, repeated diameter readings, or a clearer method.

### V06S06 — Use uncertainty to make decisions

**On-screen**

- Budget first.
- Identify the bottleneck.
- Improve the limiting measurement.

**Narration script**

The purpose of an uncertainty budget is decision making. It tells you where the experiment is weak and what improvement is worth the effort. A strong error analysis therefore does not merely list possible errors; it identifies the limiting contribution and recommends a justified action.

## V07 — Treating and Graphing Experimental Data

### V07S01 — Data treatment is allowed, but must be honest

**On-screen**

- Treatment clarifies evidence.
- Manipulation hides evidence.
- The difference is traceability and justification.

**Narration script**

Data treatment is not automatically suspicious. We often need to subtract a background, average repeated readings, transform variables or plot a linearized relationship. The problem begins when treatment hides inconvenient evidence or breaks traceability. The difference is whether the operation is justified, documented and reproducible.

### V07S02 — Legitimate treatment has a physical reason

**On-screen**

- Background subtraction.
- Unit conversion.
- Averaging comparable repeated measurements.
- Model-based transformation.

**Narration script**

Legitimate treatment has a physical or methodological reason. A background reading can be subtracted if it represents a real offset. Units can be converted for consistency. Repeated measurements of the same quantity can be averaged. A variable can be transformed when the physical model justifies that transformation.

### V07S03 — Do not average unlike conditions

**On-screen**

- Different temperatures are different conditions.
- Different loads or voltages may be different states.
- Preserve independent-variable structure.

**Narration script**

A common mistake is to average values that should remain separate. If readings were deliberately taken at different temperatures, loads or voltages, those conditions are part of the experiment. Averaging them together can destroy the relationship you were trying to measure.

### V07S04 — Transform uncertainty with the data

**On-screen**

- If x is transformed, its uncertainty changes too.
- Error bars should match plotted variables.
- Do not plot transformed data with old uncertainties.

**Narration script**

When a variable is transformed, the uncertainty must be transformed with it. For example, if a length is squared or a period is squared, the uncertainty on the plotted quantity is no longer the original uncertainty. Error bars should represent the uncertainty of the variable that is actually on the graph.

### V07S05 — A good graph is an argument

**On-screen**

- Label axes with quantity and unit.
- Use error bars when relevant.
- Choose variables based on the model.
- Fit only when the model justifies it.

**Narration script**

A graph is not just a picture of points. It is an argument about the relationship between variables. Axes must show the quantity and unit. Error bars should be used when relevant. The plotted variables should come from the physical model, and fitting should be done because the model justifies it, not because software can draw a line.

### V07S06 — Every operation needs a reason

**On-screen**

- What was changed?
- Why is it justified?
- How did uncertainty change?

**Narration script**

For every data-treatment operation, ask three questions. What was changed? Why is the change physically or experimentally justified? How did the uncertainty change? If you can answer these questions clearly, the treatment is likely to be defensible.

## V08 — Getting Physics from a Graph

### V08S01 — A fit is not just a line

**On-screen**

- Slope can be a physical quantity.
- Intercept can reveal bias or missing physics.
- Residuals show what the line misses.

**Narration script**

When students fit a straight line, they often report only the gradient and stop. But a fitted graph can say much more. The slope may represent a physical quantity. The intercept may reveal a bias or missing correction. The residuals show whether the model actually follows the data.

### V08S02 — Linearisation should come from theory

**On-screen**

- Choose axes to test the model.
- Do not transform only to make a straight line.
- Keep units attached to transformed variables.

**Narration script**

Linearisation is powerful only when it comes from the theory. We choose axes so that the model predicts a straight-line relationship. Transforming data merely to obtain a straight line can be misleading if the transformation has no physical justification. The transformed variables also need correct units.

### V08S03 — Pendulum example: slope carries meaning

**On-screen**

- Plot T squared against L for a simple pendulum model.
- Slope relates to g.
- The unit of slope matters.

**Narration script**

In the simple pendulum example, plotting period squared against length gives a straight-line form when the small-angle model applies. The slope is not just a mathematical number. It is connected to gravitational acceleration through the model, and its unit must make the relationship physically meaningful.

### V08S04 — Do not force the intercept too early

**On-screen**

- Theory may predict zero intercept.
- Experiment may show non-zero intercept.
- Investigate before forcing through zero.

**Narration script**

If theory predicts a zero intercept but the fitted intercept is non-zero, do not automatically force the line through the origin. A non-zero intercept may indicate timing delay, offset, systematic bias or a limitation of the model. Forcing the fit too early can hide useful evidence.

### V08S05 — Residuals test the pattern left behind

**On-screen**

- Random residuals support the model.
- Curved residuals suggest model mismatch.
- Outliers need investigation.

**Narration script**

Residuals are the differences between the data points and the fitted model. If the residuals are randomly scattered, the model may be adequate within uncertainty. If the residuals show a curve or systematic pattern, the straight-line model may be missing something important.

### V08S06 — Extract meaning, not only numbers

**On-screen**

- Slope: physical interpretation and unit.
- Intercept: possible offset or limitation.
- Residuals: model adequacy.

**Narration script**

The goal of graph analysis is to extract meaning, not only numbers. Interpret the slope with its unit, examine the intercept instead of ignoring it, and use residuals to judge whether the model describes the data. A graph becomes physics only when these interpretations are made.

## V09 — Discussing and Reporting an Experiment Properly

### V09S01 — A conclusion is not “close enough”

**On-screen**

- Comparison needs uncertainty.
- Percentage difference alone is incomplete.
- Discussion must explain what limits the result.

**Narration script**

Many beginner reports end with the statement that the result is close enough. That is not a scientific conclusion. A meaningful comparison needs uncertainty. A percentage difference alone tells the size of a discrepancy, but not whether that discrepancy is significant relative to the measurement uncertainty.

### V09S02 — Compare discrepancy with uncertainty

**On-screen**

- Find difference from reference.
- Compare with combined uncertainty where appropriate.
- Decide whether agreement is justified.

**Narration script**

To compare with a reference value, first identify the discrepancy. Then judge that discrepancy relative to the uncertainty. If the uncertainty range reasonably covers the reference, agreement may be defensible. If the discrepancy is much larger than the uncertainty, the discussion must explain possible causes.

### V09S03 — Avoid vague “human error”

**On-screen**

- Too vague to be useful.
- Does not identify mechanism.
- Replace with specific effects.

**Narration script**

The phrase human error is usually too vague to be useful. It does not tell the reader what happened, how it affected the result, or how it could be improved. Replace it with specific mechanisms such as parallax in reading the scale, delayed stopwatch reaction, zero offset, misalignment, temperature drift or limited resolution.

### V09S04 — A strong error analysis has structure

**On-screen**

- Name the source.
- State the direction or effect.
- Estimate importance if possible.
- Suggest a targeted improvement.

**Narration script**

A strong error analysis has a structure. Name the source of uncertainty or bias. Explain the direction or effect on the result if that is known. Estimate its importance relative to other contributions where possible. Then suggest an improvement that targets the limiting factor rather than giving a generic recommendation.

### V09S05 — Report tables and graphs professionally

**On-screen**

- Caption with purpose.
- Axes and headings include units.
- Uncertainty and significant figures are consistent.
- Raw and processed data remain traceable.

**Narration script**

Professional reporting also includes the presentation of evidence. Tables and graphs need clear captions, units, uncertainty where relevant and consistent significant figures. The reader should be able to trace how raw measurements became processed values and how those values support the conclusion.

### V09S06 — The final report should answer three questions

**On-screen**

- What was found?
- How reliable is it?
- What limits or improves the result?

**Narration script**

A good laboratory report answers three final questions. What was found? How reliable is it? What limits the result, and what improvement would matter most? If the conclusion answers these questions with numbers, units and specific mechanisms, the report has moved beyond description into scientific analysis.
