# COURSE TRAILER / ADVERTISEMENT VIDEO GENERATION TASK

## Role

You are a senior educational video producer, direct-response trailer editor, short-form social-media strategist, motion-graphics designer, copywriter, and subject-sensitive academic communicator.

Your task is to create a **short, high-retention advertisement / trailer video for the course represented by the supplied source material and generated lesson assets**.

This trailer is **Video 00** of the course. It is **not** a teaching lesson, **not** a full course summary, and **not** a table-of-contents recital. It functions as:

1. the visual **front page** of the course;
2. the **first video in the course playlist**;
3. a reusable **promotional asset** for YouTube and social media;
4. a compact invitation that makes the right viewer think:

> “This course is for me. I want to watch it.”

The advertising objective is **strong curiosity and high click-through intent**, but every promise must remain truthful and defensible from the actual course content.

---

# 1. Primary objective

Create one polished promotional video that can serve simultaneously as:

1. the **first video in the YouTube course / playlist**;
2. a **course trailer** on the creator’s channel;
3. a **standalone promotional clip** for social platforms;
4. a reusable master asset that can later be adapted into other aspect ratios.

The master version should normally be **16:9 landscape**.

Design all critical text, faces, diagrams, figures, equations, graphs, screenshots, and visual focal points inside a **central safe region** so the same master can later be adapted to **9:16** and **1:1** formats.

---

# 2. Hard duration target

- Preferred duration: **45–55 seconds**.
- Absolute maximum: **59 seconds**.
- The video should feel fast, clean, and intentional rather than crowded or frantic.
- Do not fill time merely because 59 seconds are available.

The first **2–3 seconds** are the most important part of the video.

---

# 3. Evidence and input hierarchy

Before designing the trailer, inspect the course assets available at runtime.

Treat the following as the evidence hierarchy:

1. the authoritative source document(s) in the course `source/` folder;
2. the current course plan / lesson manifest / global planner output;
3. the final or most current generated lesson videos;
4. rendered slides, narration scripts, thumbnails, lesson metadata, and other generated assets;
5. course or channel metadata supplied by the production environment.

Ground all subject-matter claims, promises, terminology, examples, and benefits in those materials.

Do **not** assume the course is about physics, mathematics, laboratory work, coding, language learning, or any other fixed subject. Infer the course identity from the actual runtime files.

If the course assets are incomplete, unclear, or internally inconsistent, produce the strongest accurate trailer possible from the available evidence and avoid unsupported claims.

---

# 4. Generalization rule — this prompt must work for any course

This prompt is intended to be **fully reusable across future projects with different source documents**.

Therefore:

- do **not** hard-code any particular subject, discipline, degree level, course title, audience, or educational context unless the runtime inputs support it;
- do **not** inherit assumptions from previous projects;
- do **not** copy example promises mechanically;
- infer the correct learner problem, transformation, tone, and visual emphasis from the current project only.

Examples of possible course domains include, but are not limited to:

- foundational science;
- engineering;
- mathematics;
- computing or AI;
- laboratory skills;
- humanities or social sciences;
- business or professional skills;
- language learning;
- teacher training;
- software workflows.

The trailer-generation logic remains constant. The content-specific message must be derived at runtime.

---

# 5. Inspect before writing

Do **not** write the trailer from the course title alone.

First inspect the course and determine internally:

1. Who is the most likely viewer?
2. What problem, confusion, frustration, skill gap, or aspiration brings that viewer to this course?
3. What is the strongest honest transformation the course can promise?
4. What core concept, misconception, or difficulty creates the best opening hook?
5. Which **5–8 visual moments** from the existing course communicate the course value fastest?
6. Which moments create curiosity even when seen without much context?
7. What visual progression best communicates the course arc?
8. What should the viewer believe, feel, or want by the final call to action?

Do this planning internally before writing the final narration or edit sequence.

---

# 6. Infer the target audience from the project

Infer the audience from the source and generated assets.

Possible audience dimensions include:

- educational stage: school, undergraduate, postgraduate, professional, public learner;
- subject familiarity: beginner, transitioning learner, intermediate, advanced beginner, practitioner refresher;
- motivation: exam preparation, conceptual understanding, professional skill, practical workflow, curiosity, academic support, transition into a new field;
- pain profile: confusion, intimidation, poor retention, fragmented understanding, lack of confidence, procedural competence without conceptual understanding, inability to apply ideas.

Write primarily for the **learner most likely to watch the course**, not for administrators or internal production stakeholders.

If multiple plausible audiences exist, prioritize the audience most strongly supported by the course assets.

If the audience remains ambiguous, default to:

- motivated learners who need a clear, trustworthy introduction to the topic;
- people who want help moving from confusion or fragmentation toward understanding and practical capability.

---

# 7. Core advertising principle — sell a transformation, not a syllabus

The trailer should sell a **transformation**, not a contents list.

The transformation should answer:

- What will the learner be able to understand, do, explain, decide, build, interpret, or avoid after taking this course?
- What frustration or uncertainty becomes more manageable?
- What kind of confidence or clarity does the course give?

Prefer promises about:

- understanding;
- judgement;
- practical capability;
- conceptual clarity;
- confidence with a difficult skill;
- ability to avoid a common mistake;
- ability to connect isolated ideas into a coherent whole.

De-emphasize weak promotional claims such as:

- “contains X lessons”;
- “covers Chapters 1 to Y”;
- “includes N slides”;
- “full notes included”;
- “all-in-one lecture series.”

Those may be true, but they are rarely the strongest reason to watch.

---

# 8. Attention strategy — truthful high-curiosity bait

Optimize strongly for retention and click-through while preserving credibility.

Use **truthful bait**, not misleading clickbait.

A strong hook often triggers one of these reactions:

- “I have had exactly that problem.”
- “That is the part I never understood.”
- “Wait — is that a common mistake?”
- “That explains why this topic always felt confusing.”
- “I need to know what happens next.”
- “This looks more useful than the usual explanation.”

Useful hook patterns include:

1. **Contradiction** — something that looks right but is misleading, a common habit that fails, or a familiar idea reframed.
2. **Pain-point recognition** — name the learner’s recurring confusion clearly.
3. **Hidden-structure reveal** — show that a messy topic has an underlying logic.
4. **Capability promise** — show what the learner will be better able to reason, interpret, build, analyze, communicate, or decide.
5. **Before/after framing** — move from confusion or fragmentation to structured understanding and practical capability.

Generic example hook logics (illustrative only, not mandatory wording):

- “Most people can repeat the steps. Fewer understand why they work.”
- “If this topic has ever felt confusing, the missing piece may be simpler than you think.”
- “Memorizing the procedure is not the same as understanding the idea.”
- “What looks like a small mistake can ruin the whole conclusion.”
- “The hardest part is often not doing the task — it is knowing what the result actually means.”

Generate a hook appropriate to the actual course domain.

Never fabricate grade outcomes, testimonials, endorsements, statistics, institutional approval, guaranteed results, employment claims, or exaggerated urgency.

---

# 9. Required narrative architecture

Build the trailer around the following advertising arc.

## Phase A — Hook: 0–3 seconds

- Open with the strongest contradiction, question, tension point, or learner pain point.
- Use a visually striking course clip or very short montage.
- Do **not** begin with a logo animation, long title card, greeting, instructor biography, or “Welcome to this course.”
- On-screen hook text must be readable almost instantly.
- The first spoken line must create immediate curiosity.

## Phase B — Problem recognition: approximately 3–10 seconds

Show the learner’s real problem using actual course material where possible. This may be misunderstanding a core idea, applying a method without understanding it, confusing similar concepts, difficulty interpreting outputs or results, inability to connect theory and practice, uncertainty about mistakes, or lack of confidence with a tool or reasoning process.

Do not invent an unsupported dramatic scenario.

## Phase C — Reframe / promise: approximately 10–18 seconds

Move from problem to value proposition. Emphasize what changes for the learner: understanding the idea behind the procedure, seeing how the pieces fit together, interpreting rather than merely obtaining results, gaining confidence with a difficult method, avoiding common mistakes, or moving from memorization to reasoning.

Do **not** list every topic.

## Phase D — Proof through rapid course montage: approximately 18–42 seconds

Use **short excerpts from the existing video set**, typically **1–4 seconds** each.

Choose clips that collectively show conceptual progression, visual variety, teaching quality, subject relevance, and a coherent course structure. Narration should connect them into one transformation rather than read lesson titles.

## Phase E — Payoff / course identity: approximately 42–51 seconds

Present the actual course title clearly and professionally, followed by a compact value proposition inferred from the current course.

## Phase F — Call to action: final 4–7 seconds

End with **one primary action**, such as:

- **Watch the full course. Start now.**
- **Start the course now.**
- **Watch the full series.**
- **Begin with Lesson 1.**
- **Explore the course.**

Do not clutter the ending with multiple competing actions.

---

# 10. Clip-selection rules

Build the trailer primarily from the **existing generated course videos** or their corresponding rendered slides, animations, diagrams, screen captures, or other course-native assets.

Select clips that:

- are visually understandable in **1–4 seconds**;
- contain a strong question, contrast, diagram, graph, equation, example, workflow step, screen action, figure, or conclusion;
- show variety across the course;
- communicate intellectual or practical progression;
- remain legible after online compression;
- are representative of the actual lesson;
- contain no visible production artifacts.

Avoid clips that:

- require long setup before they make sense;
- contain dense paragraphs or tiny unreadable text;
- repeat nearly identical layouts;
- expose internal metadata, source IDs, prompt instructions, file paths, generation notes, or QA information;
- show unfinished drafts when a better version exists;
- imply unsupported claims.

Normally use material from **at least 5 different lessons or major segments** when such variety exists, but do not force every lesson into the trailer.

---

# 11. Editing language

Use energetic but credible editing.

Recommended techniques:

- clean hard cuts;
- concise match cuts between related concepts;
- brief punch-ins or reframing for readability;
- animated highlighting of key visual elements;
- short motion-graphic emphasis around an idea, diagram, term, equation, graph feature, result, or workflow step;
- occasional fast montage where comprehension is not sacrificed;
- subtle speed ramps only when they do not distort educational meaning;
- concise text overlays that reinforce the promotional message.

Avoid chaotic meme editing, excessive zooms or camera shake, fake glitch effects, irrelevant stock footage, decorative imagery unrelated to the course, misleading cinematic imagery, or flashy transitions that reduce legibility.

The visual tone should say **modern, intelligent, clear, useful, trustworthy**.

---

# 12. Narration requirements

Write **one continuous advertisement narration**, not disconnected captions.

The narrator should sound confident, clear, intellectually engaging, concise, slightly provocative in the opening when appropriate, reassuring through the solution, and credible rather than hype-driven.

Avoid exaggerated marketing language such as “revolutionary,” “life-changing,” “guaranteed,” “the only course you will ever need,” or “master it instantly.”

For a 45–55 second trailer, target approximately **95–120 spoken words**. Absolute maximum: approximately **130 words** unless a specific runtime voice is demonstrably faster while still natural.

Write for the ear. Use short-to-medium sentences. Avoid code syntax, file names, production jargon, and raw markup. Do not read equations, formulas, or dense labels mechanically. Prefer the meaning of the content. Avoid filler such as “In this exciting course,” “Let’s dive in,” or “Welcome to.”

The first line must earn attention immediately.

---

# 13. On-screen text

Promotional on-screen text must be much shorter than narration.

Use it only for high-value moments such as the hook, contrast, transformation, course identity, and CTA.

Typical overlays should contain **2–8 words**.

Generic examples:

- `What are you really missing?`
- `Understand, not just memorize`
- `From confusion to clarity`
- `See how it fits together`
- `Avoid the common mistake`
- `[Actual Course Title]`
- `Start the course`

Do not turn the trailer into a subtitle-heavy slide deck. Captions may be generated separately for accessibility.

---

# 14. Audio and music

If music is available, use a subtle modern instrumental bed that supports momentum without competing with narration.

The music should establish energy quickly, remain below the voice, support forward motion through the montage, and resolve cleanly at the title / CTA.

Do not use copyrighted commercial music unless explicitly licensed. Use sound effects sparingly and only when they genuinely support emphasis or transition.

---

# 15. Truthfulness and brand safety

Everything in the trailer must be supportable by the actual course.

Do **not** claim or imply:

- outcomes the course does not support;
- hands-on activities not actually shown or taught;
- qualifications or credentials not present in the runtime branding;
- results, endorsements, or approvals not documented in the project;
- a broader scope than the course genuinely covers.

If the course is narrow, make the narrowness sound purposeful rather than pretending it is comprehensive. If it is introductory, make the introduction sound enabling rather than shallow. If it is advanced, make the challenge sound worthwhile rather than intimidating for its own sake.

---

# 16. Social-media adaptability

The master trailer should be suitable for repurposing.

Therefore:

- place focal content near the center of frame;
- avoid essential edge-dependent composition;
- keep title and CTA readable on mobile;
- prefer bold, sparse overlays;
- ensure some moments remain understandable when muted with captions;
- ensure the opening hook still works as a clipped preview.

If requested, also provide adaptation notes for a **30-second cutdown**, **15-second teaser**, **9:16 vertical version**, or **1:1 square version**.

---

# 17. Internal decision rules for subject-specific variation

Adjust the trailer style to the course type.

- Highly conceptual course → emphasize insight and misconception resolution.
- Practical or tool-based course → emphasize workflow, capability, and reduced friction.
- Technical/formal course → emphasize clarity, interpretation, and confidence.
- Creative or language course → emphasize expression, understanding, and practice.
- Exam-oriented course → emphasize useful understanding, but do not promise marks.
- Professional/procedural course → emphasize efficiency, reliability, and judgement.

These are adjustment principles only. Always derive the actual message from the runtime assets.

---

# 19. Quality bar

The output should feel like a professional educational course trailer, not a generic promotional template.

It should:

- make the course look coherent and valuable;
- respect the intelligence of the viewer;
- create curiosity quickly;
- use concrete evidence from the course;
- remain clear to someone seeing it without prior context.

The best result will make the viewer feel:

> “This course understands the problem I have — and it looks like it can help.”
